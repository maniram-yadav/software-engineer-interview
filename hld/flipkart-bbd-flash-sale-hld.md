# E-Commerce Flash Sale Event System (Flipkart Big Billion Days-style) — High-Level Design

**Assumed metrics** (call out if different): baseline ~5-10M concurrent shoppers on a normal day · flash-sale opening spike to 10-50x baseline within minutes · catalog/browse reads in the millions of RPS at peak · checkout/order writes far lower in volume but far higher in correctness stakes · a small number of flagship deal SKUs see extreme, concentrated contention (potentially millions of concurrent buy-attempts against a few thousand units of stock) · multi-region, CDN-heavy, AWS-primary.

**Scope, explicitly enumerated**: browsing/catalog serving under extreme read load · search · cart · a fair, capacity-matched admission mechanism for the traffic spike itself (the "virtual waiting room") · inventory reservation that never oversells, even under the single-SKU hot-key contention scenario described above · checkout and order creation · payment processing · post-sale order fulfillment handoff · real-time sales/ops analytics dashboards.

**The problem this design is actually built around**: this isn't "e-commerce, but bigger" — it's a system that must survive a demand curve shaped like a cliff (near-zero traffic to 10-50x peak within seconds of a sale opening) while enforcing a hard, unforgiving correctness constraint (a limited-stock item must never be sold to more buyers than exist units) under exactly the kind of extreme single-key write contention that breaks naive designs. Several patterns already established in this conversation apply directly — CDN/edge caching (DNS/file-upload designs), the saga pattern for multi-step atomic-in-effect transactions (banking design), idempotent, durability-first commits (banking, chat, RDBMS designs) — but the traffic-shaping and hot-inventory problems below are genuinely specific to this domain and get the real design attention.

# High-Level Design (HLD)

## 1. Scope & System Estimation

**Functional Requirements**
- Serve product catalog, search, and deal pages to an enormous, spiky concurrent audience without falling over at the moment traffic matters most
- Let users browse and add items to a cart during the sale
- Fairly and predictably admit users into the checkout/purchase flow at a rate the backend can actually sustain, rather than letting the full spike hit inventory and payment systems directly
- Reserve inventory for a limited-stock item atomically and correctly — no overselling, even under extreme concurrent contention on a single popular SKU
- Process checkout: apply discounts/coupons, calculate final price, create an order, charge payment — atomically in effect, even though it spans several independent subsystems
- Release reserved-but-unpurchased inventory back to the pool if a user abandons checkout or payment fails, within a bounded time window
- Hand off successfully placed orders to fulfillment/logistics (outside this design's core scope, but the interface point matters)
- Provide real-time visibility into sales velocity, inventory depletion, and system health during the event, since operational decisions (e.g., manually throttling a specific SKU) may need to happen live during the sale

**Non-Functional Requirements**
- Availability: the system must survive its own success — the worst-case failure mode for an event like this is the site going down at the exact moment demand is highest, which is a fundamentally different availability challenge than steady-state e-commerce traffic
- **Consistency: split, deliberately, along the same lines as every other design in this conversation** — catalog/browse data is heavily AP-leaning (a product page a few seconds stale, or a "12 left" counter that's approximate, are both fine); **inventory reservation and payment are CP, non-negotiable**, for the same reason the banking ledger was CP: overselling or double-charging isn't a minor staleness issue, it's a broken promise to a paying customer and a real financial/legal problem.
- Fairness: under an admission-controlled queue (detailed in §2), the ordering of who gets a chance to buy needs to be perceived as fair (e.g., strictly FIFO by arrival, not exploitable by refreshing the page or scripting) — a subtle but real product-trust requirement specific to flash sales.
- Scalability: the read (catalog/browse) and write (checkout/order) paths need to scale completely independently, since their load profiles and correctness requirements are entirely different — conflating them, as in every other design in this conversation, would force the wrong trade-off on at least one side.

**Back-of-the-Envelope Estimation**
- If normal peak is a few million concurrent shoppers and the sale opening spikes 10-50x, the system needs to absorb a demand curve that can go from baseline to unprecedented-peak in under a minute — this rules out any capacity plan that relies on reactive auto-scaling alone (auto-scalers typically take minutes to provision new capacity, which is too slow for a spike shaped like this); the mitigations are **aggressive pre-scaling ahead of the known sale start time** (this is a scheduled event, not an unpredictable viral spike, which is a real planning advantage) plus **admission control that deliberately smooths the effective load the backend sees**, detailed in §2.
- Hot-SKU contention: if a single flagship deal has, say, 5,000 units of stock and 2 million people attempt to buy it within the first few seconds of the sale opening, that's a **400:1 contention ratio on what is, naively, a single row/counter** — this single number is the reason inventory management for flash-sale items cannot be architected the same way as ordinary, non-contended inventory (a normal SKU with thousands of units and modest demand can just use a simple atomic decrement; a flash-sale hero SKU cannot, without a specific mitigation detailed in §2).
- Read:write ratio: catalog/browse/search traffic outnumbers actual checkout attempts by a wide margin even during a sale (most visitors browse far more than they buy, and of those who do try to buy a hot item, the overwhelming majority won't succeed given the contention ratio above) — this reinforces the same "reads must be served from cache/CDN, essentially never hitting a live database" principle used throughout this conversation, just under far more extreme load than any prior design.
- CDN/edge offload: the vast majority of catalog page weight (images, static product descriptions, prices that don't change per-request) can be served from CDN edge caches, meaning the origin/application tier only ever needs to handle the genuinely dynamic slice of traffic (personalization, real-time stock counts, cart/checkout) — this ratio is what makes the "millions of RPS" number in the estimation tractable at the origin at all.

## 2. System Architecture & Components

**Architecture Style**: Microservices, cleanly split along the same read-path/write-path fault line used in the social-platform and loyalty designs, but with one addition that's specific to this domain and doesn't appear elsewhere in this conversation: a **Virtual Waiting Room / Admission Control layer** sitting in front of the checkout path specifically, whose entire job is to convert an unpredictable, instantaneous demand spike into a smooth, backend-manageable arrival rate. Justification: every other design in this conversation assumed the backend could scale (elastically or via sharding) to meet demand; here, the demand for one specific resource (a fixed quantity of a hot SKU) is *structurally* capped regardless of how much backend capacity exists — no amount of horizontal scaling lets you sell more units of a sold-out phone. The right response to that isn't "scale harder," it's "control the rate at which people are even allowed to attempt the purchase," which is a genuinely different kind of architectural component than anything built earlier in this thread.

**Component Breakdown**
- **CDN/Edge Layer**: caches static and semi-static catalog content (product images, descriptions, category pages) as close to users as possible — the first and largest line of defense against the read-traffic spike, directly reusing the CDN role from the file-upload and DNS designs
- **Catalog & Search Service**: serves dynamic-but-cacheable product/search data, heavily fronted by application-level caching (short-TTL cache for prices/stock-level indicators, which are allowed to be approximate on the browse page even though the actual reservation step is not)
- **Cart Service**: manages per-user cart state — read/write moderate volume, not on the extreme-contention hot path
- **Virtual Waiting Room / Admission Control Service**: the traffic-shaping centerpiece of this design — when a user attempts to proceed to checkout (especially for a known hot-demand item or during peak load generally), they're issued a queue position/token rather than proceeding directly; the service releases users into the actual checkout flow at a rate matched to backend capacity, not at the rate they arrived — detailed further below and structurally similar in spirit to a rate limiter (reusing the LB/API Gateway's token-bucket concept), but operating at the scale of "control admission into a whole flow," not just "cap requests per consumer"
- **Inventory Reservation Service**: the correctness-critical core — atomically reserves a unit of stock for a checkout attempt, specifically engineered to survive the hot-SKU contention scenario from §1 (detailed below)
- **Pricing & Promotions Engine**: applies discounts/coupons/flash-sale pricing at checkout time — read-heavy against slowly-changing promotion rules, doesn't itself need the same contention-handling as inventory
- **Order Service**: creates the order record and orchestrates the checkout saga (inventory reservation → payment → order confirmation), reusing the banking design's saga pattern directly, since this is structurally the same "multiple independently-owned resources must appear to change atomically together" problem as a cross-shard bank transfer
- **Payment Service**: integrates with external payment gateways/processors, with the same idempotent, durability-first commit discipline as the banking ledger design (a payment must never be double-charged, and a successful charge must never be silently lost)
- **Notification Service**: order confirmations, "your item sold out" / "your queue turn has come" notifications — same observer/pub-sub role as the chat and social-platform designs' notification components
- **Fulfillment Handoff**: publishes confirmed orders to the logistics/fulfillment system (out of this design's core scope, but the clean interface boundary matters for keeping the flash-sale-specific concerns isolated from ordinary post-purchase operations)
- **Real-Time Ops Analytics**: Lambda-architecture (streaming + batch), same structural role as the loyalty and social-platform analytics pipelines, here surfacing sales velocity, per-SKU depletion rate, and system health metrics live during the event — critical specifically because flash sales often need live operational intervention (e.g., manually pausing sale of a SKU that's about to hit a data-integrity edge case), which is a use case none of the earlier analytics designs needed to support in real time to the same degree.

**Data Flow Walkthrough**

*Read path (browsing during the sale, the overwhelming majority of traffic):*
1. Client requests a product/category/deals page → served from CDN edge cache for the static majority of the content.
2. The genuinely dynamic slice (current stock-level indicator, personalized recommendations) is served from the Catalog Service's own aggressively-cached layer — stock-level indicators shown during browsing are explicitly allowed to be approximate (an AP-leaning "X left" counter, not the authoritative reservation count), the same "approximate is fine for display, exact is required for the actual transaction" split used by the gaming leaderboard's rank-display-vs-scatter-gather-rank distinction.

*Write path (attempting to buy — the contention-critical path):*
1. User adds to cart and proceeds to checkout. If the item is a known high-demand SKU or the system is under heavy load generally, the request is routed through the **Virtual Waiting Room**: the user receives a queue token and an estimated wait, rather than hitting the Inventory Reservation Service directly.
2. The Admission Control Service releases queued users into the actual checkout flow at a controlled rate — calibrated to what the Inventory Reservation and Payment services can sustain, not to how fast people are arriving. This is the mechanism that converts an unmanageable instantaneous spike into a manageable, roughly steady stream at the point where correctness actually matters.
3. Once admitted, the user's checkout attempt reaches the Inventory Reservation Service, which attempts an atomic reservation (detailed below) — success moves them into the Order/Payment saga; failure (sold out) returns a clear, immediate "sold out" response rather than leaving the user in an ambiguous state.
4. Order Service orchestrates the saga: inventory already reserved → charge payment → on success, confirm the order and finalize the inventory decrement; on payment failure, the Inventory Reservation Service's compensating action releases the reserved unit back to the pool (mirroring the banking design's saga compensating-transaction pattern exactly, just with "inventory units" instead of "money" as the resource being coordinated across steps).
5. Reserved-but-not-yet-purchased inventory carries a short TTL (e.g., a few minutes, matching the checkout window) — if the user abandons checkout or payment doesn't complete in time, the reservation expires and the unit becomes available again automatically, the same hold/expiry mechanism as the banking design's funds-availability holds.

## 3. Storage & Data Strategy (brief, since this HLD stops short of full LLD)

- **Catalog data**: heavily cached (CDN + application cache), backed by a read-optimized store — same AP-leaning, cache-first philosophy as every high-read-ratio system in this conversation.
- **Inventory — the correctness-critical store**: for ordinary SKUs, a straightforward atomic-decrement-with-versioning pattern (the same optimistic-concurrency approach as the loyalty ledger) is sufficient. **For hot flagship SKUs specifically, the stock counter itself is sharded** — e.g., 5,000 units split across 50 shards of 100 units each — so contention is distributed across many independently-lockable counters rather than a single global one; a purchase attempt is routed to one shard (randomly or via a lightweight round-robin), and only if that specific shard is depleted does the system fall back to checking others. This trades a small amount of "might reject an attempt while other shards still have stock" imprecision for a massive reduction in single-counter lock contention — an explicit, deliberate accuracy-vs-throughput trade-off unique to the extreme-hot-key scenario this domain creates, distinct from the banking ledger's "never approximate" stance, because the two systems face genuinely different contention profiles (a bank account isn't attacked by a million simultaneous debit attempts the way a flash-sale SKU is).
- **Order/Payment data**: strongly consistent, durable, saga-coordinated — architecturally identical in rigor to the banking ledger design, since a placed order and a completed payment carry exactly the same "must never be wrong or silently lost" bar as a financial transaction.
- **Admission-control queue state**: fast, ephemeral KV store tracking each user's queue position/token — AP-leaning, since a queue position being briefly stale just means a slightly-off wait-time estimate, not a correctness violation.

## 4. Cross-Cutting Concerns & Trade-offs

**CAP Theorem & Trade-offs**
- Catalog/browse data and displayed stock counters: **AP**, for the same reasons as every high-read-ratio system in this conversation.
- **Inventory reservation and payment: CP**, non-negotiable, for the same reasons as the banking ledger — but achieved *differently* here because the contention profile is different: banking's CP guarantee has to hold under normal, modest concurrency per account; flash-sale inventory's CP guarantee has to hold under the extreme, deliberately-adversarial-feeling contention of a hot SKU, which is exactly why sharding the hot counter (rather than just "use a database transaction," which would be correct but would collapse under the contention ratio in §1) is the specific mitigation this domain needs that banking didn't.
- **Admission control is itself a trade-off between fairness and raw throughput**: releasing users strictly FIFO is the fairest policy but isn't necessarily the policy that maximizes checkout completion rate (a strict FIFO queue might release a user who's since become distracted/left, wasting a slot); real systems commonly use a bounded-time "your turn — you have N minutes to act or lose your slot" window, which is the queue-admission analog of the inventory reservation's own short-TTL hold.

**Resiliency & Security**
- **Graceful, honest degradation over silent failure**: if a user's purchase attempt fails because the item just sold out (lost the contention race), the correct response is an immediate, clear "sold out" — never a spinner that eventually times out, and never a false "success" that gets reversed later, both of which would be a serious trust violation at exactly the moment a customer is trying to pay.
- **Bot/scalper mitigation**: the Admission Control layer is also the natural enforcement point for anti-bot measures (CAPTCHA-style challenges before issuing a queue token, per-account/per-device rate limiting on queue-join attempts) — reusing the token-bucket rate-limiting pattern from the LB/API Gateway designs, applied here to "prevent one actor from acquiring a disproportionate number of queue slots" rather than "prevent one API consumer from exceeding its quota."
- **Idempotency throughout the checkout saga**: every step (reserve inventory, charge payment, create order) carries an idempotency key, exactly the same discipline as the banking ledger and chat message designs — critical here specifically because a stressed, high-latency system under peak load is exactly the condition under which clients are most likely to retry a request that actually already succeeded.
- **Payment gateway circuit breaking**: if an external payment processor degrades under the load of a sale event, the Payment Service should circuit-break to a secondary processor or fail closed with a clear message, rather than let slow/failing payment calls back up and exhaust the Order Service's own capacity — the same fail-fast, don't-let-a-degraded-dependency-cascade principle used in the API Gateway design's backend circuit breakers.

---

### Key design decisions worth flagging back to you
1. **The Virtual Waiting Room is the one genuinely novel component in this design** — every other system in this conversation assumed backend capacity could eventually be made to meet demand through scaling or sharding; here, the demand for one specific limited resource is structurally capped no matter how much infrastructure exists, so the right architectural answer is controlling admission rate, not chasing capacity.
2. **Sharding the hot-SKU counter is a deliberate, scenario-specific accuracy-vs-contention trade-off**, distinct from the banking ledger's "never approximate" stance — the two systems both need CP correctness, but flash-sale inventory faces a contention profile banking never does, and that difference is what justifies a different mechanism even under the same consistency requirement.
3. **The read/write split here is more extreme than in any earlier design** — millions of RPS on the browse side against a comparatively tiny, correctness-critical checkout stream — which is why CDN/cache offload for reads and admission-controlled, saga-coordinated correctness for writes have to be treated as almost entirely separate engineering problems rather than points on one scaling curve.
